Topic 1.8 Graphical Representations of Summary Statistics for One Quantitative Variable

From five numbers to a picture. A dotplot shows individual observations. A boxplot compresses a numerical distribution into a picture of its center, middle half, extremes, and potential outliers.

Learning targets (Fall 2026 AP Statistics): construct a five-number summary and its boxplot (1.8.A; Skill 3.A), and describe mean–median patterns in approximately symmetric and skewed distributions (1.8.B; Skill 4.A). See the official Course and Exam Description, printed page 42.

Why compress a dotplot?

Imagine nine hypothetical workshop students whose one-way travel times, in minutes, are 5, 8, 10, 12, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18. These are invented teaching observations, not actual Maple Hill School data and not the separate 24-commute dataset in Topic 1.5. A dotplot retains each time. A boxplot makes it easier to compare a few important numerical landmarks, though it loses some detail.

Before calculating: predict which values bracket approximately the middle half of the nine journeys. What happens to the box if we change only the largest time?

1 · The five-number summary

Sort the data first. Record the minimum, first quartile ((Q_1)), median, third quartile ((Q_3)), and maximum in that order:

(minimum,Q1,median,Q3,maximum). (\text{minimum},\ Q_1,\ \text{median},\ Q_3,\ \text{maximum}).

The middle observation of our nine sorted travel times is the fifth, 12 minutes, so the median is 12. We use the median-of-halves convention from Topic 1.7: for odd sample sizes, exclude the overall median when forming the two halves. The lower half 5, 8, 10, 12 has quartile (Q_1=(8+10)/2=9) minutes. The upper half 14, 15, 16, 18 has (Q_3=(15+16)/2=15.5) minutes. Therefore,

(5,9,12,15.5,18)minutes. \boxed{(5,\ 9,\ 12,\ 15.5,\ 18)\ \text{minutes}}.

A numerical five-number summary should identify the measured variable and its units. Never report these figures as measurements of the full school population: they describe only the nine hypothetical travel times.

Why these five numbers? — A convention, not a theorem

The extremes locate the endpoints, and the three quartiles divide an ordered sample into approximately four equal-count portions. Some software uses interpolation or includes the overall median in both halves. These definitions may disagree for small samples. No universal mathematical proof selects one quartile convention. State the method and apply it consistently to the table, graph, and worked solutions.

The interquartile range (IQR) is the length of the interval occupied by the middle half:

IQR=Q3−Q1=15.5−9=6.5 minutes. IQR=Q_3-Q_1=15.5-9=\boxed{6.5\text{ minutes}}.

This is a difference in minutes, not the number of students between the quartiles. It is a measure of spread (introduced in Topic 1.7), whereas the median measures center.

Why subtract the quartiles? — Definition and interpretation

The length of any numerical interval from a to b is b−a. Because the box extends from Q1 to Q3, its numerical width equals Q3−Q1 by definition. This middle-half spread is usually less affected by changing an extreme observation than a range or standard deviation. It is a definition, not a universal claim that all datasets contain exactly half their distinct values between the quartiles.

Quick checkpoint

Suppose the sorted data are 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 13. The overall median is 7. Excluding it, the lower triple has median 4 and upper triple has median 10. The summary is (2, 4, 7, 10, 13), not (2, 5, 7, 9, 13). When you form quartiles, do not confuse the half boundaries with the middle observation of each half.

2 · Drawing a boxplot in five decisions

A boxplot is not a bar chart. Its axis is numerical, and distances along that axis have real measurement units. Follow these steps.

  1. Sort the observations and calculate the five-number summary.
  2. Screen for potential outliers using the chosen rule and identify the most extreme observations that are not flagged.
  3. Draw and label a numerical scale containing the observed range.
  4. Draw a box from (Q_1) to (Q_3), with a median stroke inside it.
  5. Draw whiskers from the box to the most extreme actual non-outlying data values and mark any outliers with separate points or asterisks.

For a modified (Tukey-style) boxplot, the lower and upper 1.5-IQR fences are

L=Q1−1.5(IQR),U=Q3+1.5(IQR). L=Q_1-1.5(IQR),\qquad U=Q_3+1.5(IQR).

An observation is flagged only if it lies strictly outside the interval from (L) to (U). Without outliers, the whiskers reach the actual minimum and maximum. With outliers, the five-number summary still retains the original minimum and maximum, but the modified boxplot whiskers stop earlier.

Why 1.5 times the IQR? — Optional screening convention

Optional enrichment — this mathematical rationale is not required for AP Statistics. The 1.5 multiplier is a widely used rule of thumb for observations distant from the middle half. The formula extends the central interval by 1.5 copies of its length on each side. It is not a theorem, cannot prove a flagged value is erroneous, and can disagree with the two-standard-deviation rule. There is no mathematical proof that 1.5 is the uniquely correct cutoff.

Critical distinction: A fence is a screening boundary, not necessarily an observed value. Never draw a whisker to a computed fence unless a genuine non-outlying observation happens to equal that fence. Even a negative lower fence can be valid as a calculation for a nonnegative variable; it is not a claimed negative observation.

Worked construction · one unusually long trip

Change the last journey from 18 to 50 minutes. The sorted sample becomes 5, 8, 10, 12, 12, 14, 15, 16, 50. Its five-number summary is (5, 9, 12, 15.5, 50). The IQR is still 6.5 minutes, and the fences are

L=9−1.5(6.5)=−0.75,U=15.5+1.5(6.5)=25.25. L=9-1.5(6.5)=-0.75,\qquad U=15.5+1.5(6.5)=25.25.

Because 50 > 25.25, 50 minutes is a potential outlier. In a modified boxplot, draw the box from 9 to 15.5 with the median at 12; draw the left whisker to 5 and the right whisker to 16, the largest observed non-outlier. Then draw a separate outlier marker at 50. The right whisker goes to 16, not 25.25 and not 50.

Why does the right whisker end at 16? — Modified-boxplot definition

List all observations at or below the upper fence: 5, 8, 10, 12, 12, 14, 15, 16. By the modified-boxplot rule, the right whisker reaches the largest such actual observation, 16. The 50-minute value receives a separate mark. This is a construction convention; an unmodified min-to-max boxplot would end its whisker at 50 instead, so specify which graph is used.

What does the box represent?

The Q1-to-Q3 box spans approximately the central 50% of observations, not necessarily half the numerical range. The left and right whisker segments typically encompass approximately a quarter of the ordered observations apiece if no outliers are present. Ties and finite-sample quartile conventions complicate exact counts; do not claim each section contains exactly 25% of distinct values. A long whisker indicates more numerical spread in that portion, not automatically more students.

4 · Reading and interpreting boxplots

The median stroke locates the middle ranked observation; it is not the mean. The box length on the numerical axis equals the IQR. A longer box means greater spread among roughly the middle half of the measured values. A longer right whisker or a right-side outlier may indicate a long right tail; the evidence should be checked with the underlying distribution.

Equal percentages do not mean equal distances. The quarters of an ordered sample may lie within numerical intervals of different widths. A boxplot does not show the sample size unless it is supplied in a label. It usually cannot reveal whether the original values form one cluster, two distinct clusters, or a gap.

Two datasets with the same five-number summary can look very different as dotplots because the five landmarks do not specify where every observation lies. A boxplot is useful for compact comparisons of position and spread, whereas the dotplots and histograms in Topic 1.5 are better for detecting modes and gaps.

In the studio, the two separated journey clusters scenario places several values at low times and another group at higher times. The finished box contains no direct label for “two peaks.” The dotplot should still be consulted.

An AP-style contextual interpretation

Suppose one sample of daily travel times has median 12 minutes and IQR 6.5 minutes. A defensible claim is: “The typical middle travel time in this sample was 12 minutes, and the central half of observed trips covered a spread of about 6.5 minutes.” It would be incorrect to say “every trip was within 6.5 minutes of the median,” to claim 6.5 students were in the box, or to generalize to all students without a sampling argument.

Checkpoint: If two boxes have equal IQR but different medians, which feature changed? Their centers differ, but their central-half spread is equal. To compare, use the same numerical axis and units and check outliers separately.

Mean versus median: a helpful, nonabsolute pattern

The mean uses every observed value, whereas the median depends on rank. In a roughly symmetric distribution, mean and median are typically close. In a right-skewed distribution, the mean is usually larger than the median; in a left-skewed distribution, the mean is usually smaller. This is a tendency, not a logical equivalence: mean > median does not prove right skew, and close values do not prove symmetry. Interpret shape with a dotplot or histogram whenever possible.

Recall the sample mean formula from Topic 1.7:

x‾=∑i=1nxin. \bar x = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}{n}.

Why does changing one value move the mean? — Optional algebra

Optional enrichment — this derivation is not required for AP Statistics. If a single observation increases by d, the sum increases by d; the new mean becomes (S+d)/n = S/n+d/n. The original times sum to 110, giving 110/9 = 12.22 minutes. Changing 18 to 50 increases the total by 32, so the mean rises by 32/9 ≈ 3.56 to 142/9 ≈ 15.78 minutes. The middle observation remains 12, so the median does not change. This is an algebraic statement about the mean, not a universal test of shape.

Right-tail example. The original times have mean 12.22 minutes and median 12 minutes. With 50 replacing 18, the mean rises to 15.78 minutes, while the median stays 12. The resulting long right tail has pulled the mean above the median.

Left-tail example. For the hypothetical times 1, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39 minutes, the median is 35, but the total is 285, giving mean 285/9 ≈ 31.67 minutes. One unusually low value pulls the mean below the median. Remember: the relative positions of mean and median are evidence about skewness, but the complete distribution is needed to assess shape reliably.

Think carefully: Does the median ever lie outside the box? No—the two quartile boundaries enclose the middle ranked position. Does the mean have to be inside the box? No. It may lie outside when sufficiently extreme observations shift it.

5 · Released exam connection

The final section discusses a released exam question and provides original practice.

The official 2019 AP Statistics FRQ 1(b)–(c) tests outlier screening and boxplot construction.

The released 2019 exam, Question 1 and its official scoring guide give the university room-size problem. For this independent walkthrough, the five-number summary in square feet is (134, 174, 253.5, 292, 315). The IQR is 118; the two 1.5-IQR fences are −3 and 469. Neither extreme is an outlier. Draw a box from 174 to 292, a median line at 253.5, and whiskers at the actual observed extrema, 134 and 315.

The released histogram has two clusters that the boxplot does not directly reveal. That comparison is a key reason to examine both a distribution plot and a numerical-summary plot. The calculation above is independent teaching commentary, not a reproduction of the official question or graph.

Common mistakes and a final synthesis

The median stroke is not the mean. A computed fence is not a whisker endpoint. A box length measures numerical middle-half spread, not the number of distinct observations. A modified boxplot with an outlier still has a five-number summary that records the actual minimum and maximum. Always report the measured variable, group and units, and consult a dotplot or histogram when shape matters.
