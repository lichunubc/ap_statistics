Topic 1.9 Comparisons of the Distributions for One Quantitative Variable

Comparing distributions is more than comparing two averages. If two groups have different centers, spreads, shapes, or unusual observations, the complete evidence matters. This chapter joins numerical summaries, common-scale graphs, contextual conclusions, and standardized z-scores.

Learning targets (Fall 2026 CED): compare graphical distributions (1.9.A), compare their summary statistics (1.9.B), justify claims using multiple graphs (1.9.C), calculate population-based z-scores (1.9.D), and compare relative positions using z-scores (1.9.E). Required skills: 4.A, 4.B, 3.B, 4.C. Official College Board course framework, printed pp. 43–44.

1 · Make a fair, useful comparison

A transport planner wishes to compare one-way journey time on two hypothetical bus routes. The variable—time in minutes—is the same quantitative variable for both groups, and each group contains 12 invented observations:

Route A (minutes) Route B (minutes)
12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 16, 17, 17, 18, 18, 19, 21 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 38

These are original simulated teaching values, not measurements from actual bus riders or Maple Hill School students. They are unrelated to the separate School A categorical dataset and the earlier 24-commute dataset.

Before looking at averages, identify what each route’s list suggests about typical journey time and whether one route has an unusually long journey. Then consider whether “Route B is always faster” is supported. A distribution allows comparisons of groups, not guarantees about individual journeys.

What belongs in a comparison?

Use the same x-axis scale, units, bin widths, and (when appropriate) frequency or relative-frequency conventions. Describe:

  • Shape: approximately symmetric, right- or left-skewed, unimodal, bimodal, or roughly uniform. Identify the direction of a longer tail, not the side with the taller bar.
  • Center: compare medians (and means if informative), with actual values and units.
  • Spread: compare IQRs, standard deviations, ranges, or other appropriate measures, explicitly stating which you use.
  • Unusual features: possible outliers, gaps, clusters, or conspicuous extremes, saying which graph provides evidence.

A histogram, dotplot, or back-to-back stemplot can show detailed patterns including gaps and peaks. Side-by-side boxplots reveal quartiles, medians, middle-half spread, and flagged outliers, but do not reliably show gaps or the number of modes. A box that looks symmetric cannot prove that a distribution is unimodal or normally distributed.

Comparison must be relational. “A’s median is 16.5 minutes and B’s median is 13.5 minutes” lists facts. “A’s median journey is 3 minutes longer than B’s” compares them in context and quantitatively. A complete response also describes spread and, where useful, shape.

Worked example: routes A and B

For these 12 observations per route, we apply the same median-of-halves quartile convention introduced in Topic 1.7. We use sample standard deviations, because these are samples, not complete route populations.

Statistic Route A Route B
Sample size 12 12
Mean (minutes) 16.50 15.58
Median (minutes) 16.50 13.50
Q1 (minutes) 15.00 11.50
Q3 (minutes) 18.00 16.50
IQR (minutes) 3.00 5.00
Sample SD (minutes) 2.39 7.63
Range (minutes) 9 29

Rounded means and SDs are for communication; the interactive lab recalculates from the unrounded values. The IQR is Q3 − Q1 as developed in Topic 1.7.

Center. In these particular samples, Route A’s median trip is 16.5 minutes, 3.0 minutes longer than Route B’s 13.5 minutes. The sample means differ much less (16.50 versus 15.58) because Route B includes the unusually long 38-minute journey.

Variability. Route B’s middle-half spread is greater (IQR 5 rather than 3 minutes), and its sample SD is also much greater (7.63 versus 2.39 minutes). Reporting both is useful here: the SD responds strongly to Route B’s high extreme; the IQR focuses on the middle portion.

Shape and unusual features. Route A is fairly balanced around its center. Route B has a pronounced right tail and a large gap from 19 to 38 minutes. With its quartiles, the upper 1.5-IQR fence is 16.5 + 1.5(5) = 24 minutes, so 38 is flagged as a potential outlier by this convention. An unusual value should be investigated, not automatically deleted.

Conclusion supported by these data: Route B has the lower median journey time but noticeably greater variability in the two samples. We have not established that one route causes faster travel, that all B trips are shorter, or that the observed differences hold identically for the entire population.

Why the reported IQRs and the 1.5-IQR fence? — Definition and convention

The IQR is defined as the upper quartile minus the lower quartile: Route A has 18 − 15 = 3 minutes and Route B has 16.5 − 11.5 = 5 minutes. The upper screening fence adds 1.5 times the IQR to the third quartile, giving 16.5 + 1.5 × 5 = 24. The multiplier 1.5 is a chosen screening convention, not a universally provable boundary between valid observations and mistakes. Different quartile conventions may shift the fence slightly.

Compare responsibly

If two groups have unequal sample sizes, raw histogram or dotplot counts can be visually misleading for percentage comparisons. Use relative-frequency histograms with common bin boundaries when the investigative question concerns proportions; keep the number of observations and graph units visible. When two distributions have different numerical units (for example, exam grades out of different totals), raw values cannot be directly compared until their meanings are put on a common scale.

A stronger AP response names both groups, the quantitative variable, the units, and numerical or graphical evidence supporting the comparison. Describing does not establish causation; study design determines whether causal conclusions are justified. More guidance on design follows in Topics 1.10–1.13.

Checkpoint. If two boxplots have identical medians but one has a wider box, what can you conclude? Its middle 50% has a larger IQR. You cannot conclude which one has more peaks or which group always has larger observations. Explain why.

Static alternative: the original 12 observations for each route and their verified comparison are above; all scenarios use invented teaching data. Dotplots encode individual repeated values; boxplots encode medians/quartiles and whiskers; histograms group observations into the same five-minute intervals.

What to discover: when you replace Route B’s 38-minute trip with 20 minutes, its large high gap vanishes and its standard deviation falls sharply; its median remains 13.5 minutes. Choosing “shifted” produces groups with equal IQR and sample SD, although the center is five minutes apart. Changing to boxplots hides the detailed dot pattern. A graph should answer your question, not merely look attractive.

Why does adding a constant shift the center but not the spread? — Optional derivation

Optional enrichment — this derivation is not required for AP Statistics. Suppose every observation changes from x to x + c. The new mean is (sum of the original values + n × c)/n, so it equals the old mean plus c. Every value’s distance from the new mean is unchanged because (x + c) − (mean + c) = x − mean. Therefore the sum of squared deviations and sample standard deviation do not change. Every quartile rises by c, so Q3 − Q1 does not change either. Shifting a distribution is not the same as rescaling its variability.

3 · Relative position: the z-score

A raw score of 86 on one assessment and 90 on another does not say who performed better relative to their own distribution. We need the group’s mean and standard deviation. A z-score describes how many standard deviations an observation lies above (positive) or below (negative) its reference mean. It carries no physical units.

When population parameters are known, use

z=x−μσ,σ>0, z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}, \qquad \sigma>0,

where (x) is the observed measurement, () is its population mean, and () is its population standard deviation. Subtraction locates the score relative to the mean; division expresses that distance in standard deviation units.

Why does standardizing count standard deviations? — Optional algebraic derivation

Optional enrichment — this derivation is not required for AP Statistics. A distance of x − μ is measured in the original unit. One standard deviation is σ of those units. Dividing distance by σ tells us how many such equal-sized units separate x from the mean. For example, a difference of 16 points when σ = 8 points is two standard deviations, not sixteen. Because numerator and denominator share units, z has no units. The formula requires σ > 0; when every population value is identical, σ = 0 and this expression is undefined.

Worked example. Suppose two hypothetical standardized school assessments measure different skills. On Assessment A, a student scores 86 points, while the population has mean 70 and SD 8 points. Then (z_A=(86-70)/8=). On Assessment B the student scores 90 points, while that population has mean 80 and SD 10 points. Then (z_B=(90-80)/10=). A is the stronger relative position—two SDs above its own mean rather than one—even though 86 is the smaller raw score. This is not a claim about which assessment was harder or what percentile the student attained.

Interpret signs and compare distributions

A z-score of 0 means at the mean; −1.5 means one and a half standard deviations below the mean; +2 means two above. Whether a large positive z-score is desirable depends on the measured outcome: unusually high test scores might be desirable, while unusually long emergency wait times might not.

When two distributions are not similarly shaped, equal z-scores do not necessarily imply equal percentile ranks. A z-score is a measure of standardized distance, not a probability. Converting raw scores to z-scores does not make the distribution normal or change how its observations are ordered (for positive standard deviation).

If a question instead gives a z-score and asks for the raw value, rearrange:

x=μ+zσ. x=\mu+z\sigma.

Why can we recover the raw value? — Optional algebraic derivation

Optional enrichment — this derivation is not required for AP Statistics. Starting with z = (x − μ)/σ and σ > 0, multiply both sides by σ to get zσ = x − μ. Add μ to both sides to obtain x = μ + zσ. This is an inverse calculation, not a new probability model.

If population parameters are not known and the problem permits estimates from a sample, we can report a sample-referenced standardized score:

zsample=x−x‾s,s>0. z_{\text{sample}}=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{s}, \qquad s>0.

Why substitute sample summaries? — Conditions and limitation

This is a sample-based definition of relative position, not proof that a sample mean and SD equal their population counterparts. Replacing unknown μ and σ with the sample mean and sample SD gives a descriptive standardized distance relative to that sample. It is meaningful only when the reference group is specified and s is positive. Do not quietly use a sample mean when a population mean is supplied.

For example, in Route A’s sample a 21-minute trip has sample-referenced z approximately ((21-16.5)/2.393). In Route B’s sample a 19-minute trip has z approximately ((19-15.583)/7.633). A is farther above its own route’s typical time; this does not say that 21 minutes is a rarer percentile without comparing the shapes.

The entire lesson in one comparison: use a common-scale graph to understand shape, center, spread, and unusual features. Use a numerical summary to quantify evidence. Use a z-score when the question concerns an individual’s relative distance from their reference mean. Never substitute a z-score for a complete description or an experimental design.

Independent instructional walkthrough of part B. The released Breed H sample’s median weight is 64 pounds and its IQR is 16 pounds (Q1 56, Q3 72); the Breed J boxplot indicates a median also near 64 pounds but a larger IQR, approximately 24 pounds. H’s observed range is 32 pounds, compared with approximately 40 pounds for J. Thus the sampled goat breeds have similar medians, but J’s sampled weights have greater middle-half and overall variability. Common errors and final checklist

Do not say that a distribution with a larger mean necessarily has less variability. Do not mistake a taller bar for a longer tail. Do not treat a single boxplot as proof of normality, uniformity, or unimodality. Never write “more spread” without specifying which statistic or graphical feature supports that conclusion. For comparisons of different-sized samples, distinguish frequency from relative frequency. Do not assert population generalizations or causal mechanisms from descriptive displays alone.

For z-scores, always specify the reference distribution; check that the appropriate SD is positive, keep raw measurement units distinct from unit-free z, interpret the sign and magnitude, and remember: standardization does not produce normality or automatically identify percentiles.
