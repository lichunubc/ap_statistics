Topic 1.9 Comparisons of the Distributions for One Quantitative Variable

Comparing distributions is more than comparing two averages. If two groups have different centers, spreads, shapes, or unusual observations, the complete evidence matters. This chapter joins numerical summaries, common-scale graphs, contextual conclusions, and standardized z-scores.

1 · Make a fair, useful comparison

A transport planner wishes to compare one-way journey time on two bus routes. The variable—time in minutes—is the same quantitative variable for both groups, and each group contains 12 observations:

Route A (minutes) Route B (minutes)
12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 16, 17, 17, 18, 18, 19, 21 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 38

Before looking at averages, identify what each route’s list suggests about typical journey time and whether one route has an unusually long journey. Then consider whether “Route B is always faster” is supported. A distribution allows comparisons of groups, not guarantees about individual journeys.

What belongs in a comparison?

Use the same x-axis scale, units, bin widths, and (when appropriate) frequency or relative-frequency conventions. Describe:

  • Shape: approximately symmetric, right- or left-skewed, unimodal, bimodal, or roughly uniform. Identify the direction of a longer tail, not the side with the taller bar.
  • Center: compare medians (and means if informative), with actual values and units.
  • Spread: compare IQRs, standard deviations, ranges, or other appropriate measures, explicitly stating which you use.
  • Unusual features: possible outliers, gaps, clusters, or conspicuous extremes, saying which graph provides evidence.

A histogram, dotplot, or back-to-back stemplot can show detailed patterns including gaps and peaks. Side-by-side boxplots reveal quartiles, medians, middle-half spread, and flagged outliers, but do not reliably show gaps or the number of modes. A box that looks symmetric cannot prove that a distribution is unimodal or normally distributed.

Comparison must be relational. “A’s median is 16.5 minutes and B’s median is 13.5 minutes” lists facts. “A’s median journey is 3 minutes longer than B’s” compares them in context and quantitatively. A complete response also describes spread and, where useful, shape.

Worked example: routes A and B

For these 12 observations per route, we apply the same median-of-halves quartile convention introduced in Topic 1.7. We use sample standard deviations, because these are samples, not complete route populations.

Statistic Route A Route B
Sample size 12 12
Mean (minutes) 16.50 15.58
Median (minutes) 16.50 13.50
Q1 (minutes) 15.00 11.50
Q3 (minutes) 18.00 16.50
IQR (minutes) 3.00 5.00
Sample SD (minutes) 2.39 7.63
Range (minutes) 9 29

Rounded means and SDs are for communication; the interactive lab recalculates from the unrounded values. The IQR is Q3 − Q1 as developed in Topic 1.7.

Center. In these particular samples, Route A’s median trip is 16.5 minutes, 3.0 minutes longer than Route B’s 13.5 minutes. The sample means differ much less (16.50 versus 15.58) because Route B includes the unusually long 38-minute journey.

Variability. Route B’s middle-half spread is greater (IQR 5 rather than 3 minutes), and its sample SD is also much greater (7.63 versus 2.39 minutes). Reporting both is useful here: the SD responds strongly to Route B’s high extreme; the IQR focuses on the middle portion.

Shape and unusual features. Route A is fairly balanced around its center. Route B has a pronounced right tail and a large gap from 19 to 38 minutes. With its quartiles, the upper 1.5-IQR fence is 16.5+1.5(5)=2416.5+1.5(5)=24 minutes, so 38 is flagged as a potential outlier by this convention. An unusual value should be investigated, not automatically deleted.

Conclusion supported by these data: Route B has the lower median journey time but noticeably greater variability in the two samples. We have not established that one route causes faster travel, that all B trips are shorter, or that the observed differences hold identically for the entire population.

Why the reported IQRs and the 1.5-IQR fence? — Definition and convention

The IQR is defined as the upper quartile minus the lower quartile: Route A has 18−15=318-15=3 minutes and Route B has 16.5−11.5=516.5-11.5=5 minutes. The upper screening fence adds 1.5 times the IQR to the third quartile, giving 16.5+1.5×5=2416.5+1.5\times5=24. The multiplier 1.5 is a chosen screening convention, not a universally provable boundary between valid observations and mistakes. Different quartile conventions may shift the fence slightly.

Compare responsibly

If two groups have unequal sample sizes, raw histogram or dotplot counts can be visually misleading for percentage comparisons. Use relative-frequency histograms with common bin boundaries when the investigative question concerns proportions; keep the number of observations and graph units visible. When two distributions have different numerical units (for example, exam grades out of different totals), raw values cannot be directly compared until their meanings are put on a common scale.

A stronger AP response names both groups, the quantitative variable, the units, and numerical or graphical evidence supporting the comparison. Describing does not establish causation; study design determines whether causal conclusions are justified. More guidance on design follows in Topics 1.10–1.13.

Checkpoint. If two boxplots have identical medians but one has a wider box, what can you conclude? Its middle 50% has a larger IQR. You cannot conclude which one has more peaks or which group always has larger observations. Explain why.

The data table above gives the original 12 times for each route and their summaries. Dotplots encode individual repeated values; boxplots encode medians/quartiles and whiskers; histograms group observations into the same five-minute intervals.

What to discover: when you replace Route B’s 38-minute trip with 20 minutes, its large high gap vanishes and its standard deviation falls sharply; its median remains 13.5 minutes. Choosing “shifted” produces groups with equal IQR and sample SD, although the center is five minutes apart. Changing to boxplots hides the detailed dot pattern. A graph should answer your question, not merely look attractive.

Why does adding a constant shift the center but not the spread? — Optional derivation

Suppose each observation changes from xix_i to yi=xi+cy_i=x_i+c. The new mean is

y‾=∑i=1n(xi+c)n=x‾+c. \bar y=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i+c)}{n}=\bar x+c.

Each deviation from the new mean is unchanged:

(yi−y‾)=(xi+c)−(x‾+c)=xi−x‾. (y_i-\bar y)=(x_i+c)-(\bar x+c)=x_i-\bar x.

The squared deviations, sample variance and standard deviation are therefore unchanged. Both quartiles increase by cc, leaving the IQR unchanged:

(Q3+c)−(Q1+c)=Q3−Q1. (Q_3+c)-(Q_1+c)=Q_3-Q_1.

A shift changes the center without changing the spread.

3 · Relative position: the z-score

A score of 86 on one assessment and 90 on another does not say which is stronger relative to its own distribution. The reference mean and standard deviation provide the missing context. A z-score gives the number of standard deviations an observation lies above or below its reference mean.

For a population with mean μ\mu and standard deviation σ>0\sigma>0,

z=x−μσ. z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}.

Here xx is the observed value. Subtracting the mean gives a distance in the original units; dividing by σ\sigma converts it into standard-deviation units. A z-score is unit-free: positive above the mean, negative below it, and zero at the mean.

Derivation: distance in standard-deviation units (optional)

If an observation is zz standard deviations above the mean, its displacement is zσz\sigma. Thus

x−μ=zσ⇒z=x−μσ. x-\mu=z\sigma\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}.

The numerator and denominator share the same measurement units, so the units cancel. The condition σ>0\sigma>0 matters: with no variability, this standardized distance is undefined.

Worked example: compare two assessments

On Assessment A, a student scores 86 points. The population mean is 70 points and its standard deviation is 8 points:

zA=86−708=168=2.00. z_A=\frac{86-70}{8}=\frac{16}{8}=\boxed{2.00}.

On Assessment B, the student scores 90 points. The population mean is 80 points and its standard deviation is 10 points:

zB=90−8010=1010=1.00. z_B=\frac{90-80}{10}=\frac{10}{10}=\boxed{1.00}.

Assessment A has the stronger relative position: two standard deviations above its own mean, compared with one on B, even though its raw score is lower.

Interpret signs and compare distributions

A z-score of −1.5-1.5 means one and a half standard deviations below the reference mean; +2+2 means two standard deviations above it. Whether a larger value is desirable depends on the variable: a higher assessment score might be desirable, while a longer emergency wait time might not.

A z-score describes standardized distance, not a percentile or probability. Equal z-scores in differently shaped distributions can correspond to different percentile ranks. Standardizing preserves shape and ordering; it does not make a distribution normal.

Recover a value from its z-score

Rearrange the standardization formula:

x=μ+zσ. x=\mu+z\sigma.

Derivation: recover the raw value (optional)

Multiply both sides of z=(x−μ)/σz=(x-\mu)/\sigma by σ\sigma, then add μ\mu:

zσ=x−μ,x=μ+zσ. \begin{aligned} z\sigma&=x-\mu,\\ x&=\mu+z\sigma. \end{aligned}

For example, z=1.5z=1.5, μ=70\mu=70 points and σ=8\sigma=8 points give x=70+1.5(8)=82x=70+1.5(8)=82 points.

Describe relative position within a sample

Use the sample’s own mean x‾\bar{x} and sample standard deviation s>0s>0:

zsample=x−x‾s. z_{\mathrm{sample}}=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{s}.

Why use the sample summaries?

This defines relative position using the sample’s own center and spread. Sample summaries estimate population parameters when the sampling context supports that use; they are not automatically equal to them. State which reference group the standardized score describes.

In Route A’s sample, a 21-minute trip has

zA=21−16.52.393≈1.88. z_A=\frac{21-16.5}{2.393}\approx1.88.

In Route B’s sample, a 19-minute trip has

zB=19−15.5837.633≈0.45. z_B=\frac{19-15.583}{7.633}\approx0.45.

The Route A trip is farther above its own route’s mean in standard-deviation units. Percentile comparisons would also require the shapes of the distributions.

The entire lesson in one comparison: use a common-scale graph to understand shape, center, spread, and unusual features. Use a numerical summary to quantify evidence. Use a z-score when the question concerns an individual’s relative distance from their reference mean. Never substitute a z-score for a complete description or an experimental design.

4 · AP exam example

2026 AP Statistics, Question 1: comparing goat weights.

Worked solution: part B

Breed H has median weight 64 pounds, with Q1=56Q_1=56 and Q3=72Q_3=72, so

IQRH=72−56=16 pounds. \mathrm{IQR}_H=72-56=16\text{ pounds}.

Breed J’s boxplot indicates a median near 64 pounds and quartiles near 56 and 80 pounds, giving an IQR of approximately 24 pounds. The observed ranges are 32 pounds for H and approximately 40 pounds for J.

The sampled breeds have similar median weights, but Breed J has greater middle-half and overall variability. State the groups, variable and units, then support the comparison with numerical evidence.

Worked solution: part C

The stem-and-leaf plot shows two concentrations of Breed H weights. A boxplot records the five-number summary rather than the individual arrangement, so it does not reliably display those two peaks. The stemplot shows detail; the boxplot emphasizes center and spread.

Common errors and final checklist

  • Compare like with like. Name both groups, use the same variable and units, and use common scales. For unequal sample sizes, use relative frequencies when comparing proportions.
  • Describe more than the average. Compare center, spread, shape and unusual features. Name the statistic or graphical feature supporting each claim.
  • Choose a graph for the feature. Dotplots and histograms reveal gaps and peaks; boxplots summarize quartiles, median and spread. A boxplot alone does not establish normality or the number of modes.
  • Interpret z-scores in their reference groups. Use a positive standard deviation and explain the sign and magnitude. Standardized distance is not a percentile, and standardization does not create normality.
  • Match conclusions to the evidence. Descriptive displays show sample patterns; population and causal conclusions depend on study design.
