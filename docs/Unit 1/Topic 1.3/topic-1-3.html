Topic 1.3 Tabular Representation and Summary Statistics for One Categorical Variable

Turn individual responses into evidence. Count the observations in each category, convert those counts into relative frequencies, and use an explicitly identified denominator to justify a claim.

Learning targets: construct categorical frequency and relative-frequency tables (1.3.A; skill 3.A) and describe their numerical summaries in context (1.3.B; skill 4.A).

One variable, one response per person

School A has 1,000 students enrolled this term. In our hypothetical sample of 40 School A students, each reports a usual main travel method: Walk, Bicycle, Bus, or Car. This is the same categorical study introduced in Topic 1.1; it is not the separate sample of 24 numerical commute times used in Topic 1.5.

The investigative question is: What proportion of School A students this term usually travel mainly by bus?

For a one-response frequency table, categories should be mutually exclusive (a respondent cannot be counted in two categories) and collectively exhaustive (every valid response has a place). Here, main method means the method used for the greatest distance on a typical school day. A real questionnaire should also provide consistent rules for ties or unlisted methods; our hypothetical data specify exactly one method for each of the 40 respondents.

From tallies to frequencies

A frequency is the number of observational units in one category. Read each response, add one tally mark to exactly one row, and verify that the frequency total equals the number of classified responses. In tally notation, a fifth stroke crosses the previous four: ||||/ means five.

Main travel method Tallies (groups of five) Frequency
Walk ||||/ ||| 8
Bicycle ||||/ | 6
Bus ||||/ ||||/ ||||/ ||| 18
Car ||||/ ||| 8
Total 40

The check is 8+6+18+8=408+6+18+8=40. The sum would be larger than 40 if someone were counted twice, or smaller if some responses were missing or unclassified.

Activity: construct the table one response at a time

Predict where the next tally belongs. Advance one response, advance five, reveal the finished table, or reset. For teaching purposes, the activity reproducibly shuffles the hypothetical category totals; it does not claim to preserve an actual survey response order.

Static reference: Walk 8, Bicycle 6, Bus 18, Car 8; 40 total. The interactive tally activity requires JavaScript.

Relative frequency and percentages

A relative frequency is the category count divided by the total number of classified observations. Since every one of the 40 sampled students supplied one answer, our denominator is n=40n=40.

Relative frequency=category counttotal count,percentage=relative frequency×100%. \text{Relative frequency}=\frac{\text{category count}}{\text{total count}}, \qquad \text{percentage}=\text{relative frequency}\times100\%.

For the Bus category, 18/40=0.45=45%18/40=0.45=45\%.

Main method Count Relative frequency Percentage
Walk 8 0.20 20%
Bicycle 6 0.15 15%
Bus 18 0.45 45%
Car 8 0.20 20%
Total 40 1.00 100%

Frequency answers how many? Relative frequency answers what share of the group? Always name the group: 18 of the 40 sampled School A students reported Bus, corresponding to 45% of the sample. Do not divide 18 by the population size 1,000, and do not treat 45% as the exact percentage among all 1,000 students.

TipRounding does not create missing people

Use exact counts to calculate fractions, then round for display. Individually rounded percentages may total 99.9% or 100.1%. The exact unrounded proportions of an exhaustive single-response table still total 1.

Ratios change when the denominator changes

A part-to-whole ratio compares one category to the entire group. The ratio Bus to all responses is 18:40=9:2018:40=9:20, which represents the same share as 18/40=45%18/40=45\%.

A part-to-part ratio compares two categories. Bus to Car is 18:8=9:418:8=9:4, and 18/8=2.2518/8=2.25 means that there are 2.25 times as many sampled bus riders as sampled car riders. It does not mean that 225% of all sampled students take the bus.

Activity: the denominator explorer

Select the numerator category and the group being used as the denominator. Explain who is included in the denominator before examining the result.

Static reference: Bus among all 40 respondents is 18/40=45%18/40=45\%. Bus compared with Car is 18:8=9:418:8=9:4.

For another comparison, Bus among only Bus-or-Bicycle respondents is 18/(18+6)=18/24=75%18/(18+6)=18/24=75\%. The category count did not change; the group described did. Ratios become interpretable only when both sides are labelled.

Most common is not always a majority

A modal category is the category with the greatest frequency. Bus is the most common category because 18 exceeds the counts 8, 6, and 8. This is a plurality, not a majority: 18/40=45%18/40=45\% is below 50%.

Because Walk and Bicycle are disjoint categories, they can be combined: 8+6=148+6=14 students, or 14/40=35%14/40=35\%, reported Walk or Bicycle. Do not add counts that overlap. In a choose every method used survey, a respondent may check both Walk and Bus and the total category selections can exceed the number of respondents.

A supported statement is: “Among the 40 sampled School A students, Bus was the most common usual main travel method, reported by 18 students (45%).” An unsupported overstatement is “Exactly 45% of all 1,000 School A students travel by bus.” The sample does not reveal the exact population proportion.

Checkpoint: what does the table justify?

The four original cases begin with no answer selected. Choose, check the explanation, clear your choice, or move to the next case.

Static reference: Bus is the largest category (45%) but not a majority; Walk or Bicycle accounts for 35% of this sample.

A hypothetical movie-satisfaction survey has 30 responses: Very dissatisfied 2, Dissatisfied 4, Neutral 5, Satisfied 12, Very satisfied 7. Compute category percentages to one decimal place and the share Satisfied or Very satisfied.

The modal category is Satisfied. The two positive categories have 12+7=1912+7=19 responses out of 30: 19/30≈63.3%19/30\approx63.3\%. If categories are coded 1–5, those numbers are labels for an ordered categorical variable, not automatically an equally spaced quantitative measurement.

3. The majority mistake

A student writes, “Bus is the largest category, so the majority of respondents take the bus.” Identify exactly what is right and wrong.

Solution.

Show solution

Bus is the largest individual category: 18 exceeds 8, 6, and 8. But a majority is more than half; 18/40=45%<50%18/40=45\%<50\%. Correct report: “Bus was the most common main method among the 40 sampled students, reported by 18 (45%), but fewer than half chose it.”

4. Two different denominators

Compute (a) Bus to Bicycle as a simplified ratio, (b) Bus among all 40 responses, and (c) Bus among Bus-or-Bicycle responses.

Solution.

Show solution
  1. 18:6=3:118:6=3:1; (b) 18/40=0.45=45%18/40=0.45=45\%; (c) 18/(18+6)=18/24=0.75=75%18/(18+6)=18/24=0.75=75\%. The numerator is unchanged, while the denominator for (c) includes only the 24 sampled people in the selected pair of categories.

5. Why don’t the counts sum to 30?

A check every method used survey has 30 respondents and counts Walk 18, Bus 12, Bicycle 8, Car 7, totalling 45. Is the table necessarily wrong? Must percentages of respondents selecting each method add to 100%?

Solution.

Show solution

No. Respondents may select more than one method; the 45 selections can come from 30 people. Dividing by 30 gives 60%, 40%, 26.7%, 23.3% of respondents selecting each method, summing to approximately 150%. These are overlapping categories, not a single-answer distribution whose relative frequencies must sum to 100%.

6. A misleading denominator

A report says, “Bus accounts for 18% of School A’s 1,000 students because 18 sampled students chose Bus.” Correct the calculation and statement.

Solution.

Show solution

The appropriate denominator for the sampled bus count is 40 sampled students. The sample percentage is 18/40=45%18/40=45\%, not 18%. A defensible report is “Among 40 sampled School A students, 18 (45%) reported Bus as their usual main method.” The exact percentage among all 1,000 students is not known from these data.

7. Rounding is not missing data

Three categories each contain one person in a survey of three. The reported percentages, 33.3%, 33.3%, and 33.3%, sum to 99.9%. Has one person been left out?

Solution.

Show solution

No. 1+1+1=31+1+1=3 includes everyone. The exact proportions are 1/31/3 each and sum to 1. Rounding each 100/3%100/3\% to one decimal creates the 0.1-percentage-point difference.

Before moving on

You should now be able to construct a one-response table, compute and interpret frequency and relative frequency, identify the denominator in a ratio, distinguish most common from majority, and justify contextual claims without exceeding the data.
