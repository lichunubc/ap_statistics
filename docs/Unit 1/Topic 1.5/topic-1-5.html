Individual values

Learning goal: Construct and interpret dotplots and stem-and-leaf plots while preserving numerical order, units, and repeated values.

Start with the variable

An observational unit is one student. The variable is that student’s one-way commute time, in minutes. A distribution tells us which values occur and how often they occur.

For quantitative data, position measures an amount. The distance from 10 to 20 minutes must represent the same change as the distance from 20 to 30 minutes. We cannot reorder the numbers as we sometimes reorder unordered category labels.

Our sorted data are:

5, 8, 10, 12, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 18, 20, 20,
22, 24, 25, 26, 28, 30, 32, 35, 38, 42, 48, 58

Download the 24 recorded values as CSV.

A dotplot: one dot for every observation

Draw a labelled numerical axis. Place one dot at each recorded value. When a value repeats, stack the dots vertically. Two dots at 12 mean two students, each with a 12-minute commute—not one student with a 24-minute commute.

There are 24 dots; the recorded values 12, 18, and 20 each occur twice.

The axis extends from 0 to 60, but the observations extend from 5 to 58. Axis endpoints are not automatically observed values. A dotplot preserves recorded values, but not the original order of data collection.

A stem and a leaf reconstruct one value

For these whole-minute times, use tens digits as stems and ones digits as leaves:

28=10(2)+8,key: 2∣8=28 minutes.28=10(2)+8,\qquad \text{key: }2\mid8=28\text{ minutes}.

Write stems in increasing order. Sort the leaves within each row. Keep repeated leaves and zeros: each leaf still represents one observation. Include intermediate empty stems when necessary so a gap is not hidden.

Stem | Leaves
  0  | 5 8
  1  | 0 2 2 4 5 6 8 8
  2  | 0 0 2 4 5 6 8
  3  | 0 2 5 8
  4  | 2 8
  5  | 8
Key: 2 | 8 = 28 minutes

Read the key first. With the key 1∣2=1.21\mid2=1.2 seconds, the same printed digits would represent different measurements. A stem may contain more than one digit; a leaf is usually one digit.

Replay the construction

Choose a display, then add observations. The activity uses our 24 times in a mixed order so you can see repeated values finding their proper positions.

Check your understanding

How many students recorded at least 40 minutes? Which exact values did they record?

Show solution

There are three students, with recorded times 42, 48, and 58 minutes. Both displays preserve individual values, so the conclusion is exact at the precision of the recorded data.

TipBefore you move on

Recover a sorted list by writing one entry for every dot or leaf. Counting only unique positions would lose repeated observations.

Build a histogram

Learning goal: Choose and read bins, tally observations, and draw one complete bar for each row of the frequency table.

Step 1 · Choose and read the bins

A histogram groups quantitative observations into ordered intervals called bins. A bar’s width represents an interval of the variable; its height gives the frequency or relative frequency in that interval. We use equal-width bins throughout this lesson.

The commute times run from 5 to 58 minutes. A display spanning 0–60 minutes with width 10 covers every observation:

Boundaries: 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 minutes; six bins of width 10.

number of bins=60−010=6.\text{number of bins}=\frac{60-0}{10}=6.

Seven boundary marks produce six intervals, not seven bins. For a span SS, equal bin width ww, and number of bins kk, S=kwS=kw when the bins exactly partition the span.

Use one consistent endpoint rule

Our convention is left-inclusive and right-exclusive:

a≤x<b.a\leq x<b.

Thus 20 enters 20≤x<3020\leq x<30, not 10≤x<2010\leq x<20. Every observation must enter exactly one bin. Other conventions are possible but must be clearly stated. Here, 58 is below 60 and enters the final bin. A value equal to the final right boundary would require an explicit endpoint exception or an additional bin.

Step 3 · Draw one whole bar per table row

Start the frequency axis at zero and use an even scale. The first row has frequency 2, so draw the first bar to height 2. The next row has frequency 8, so draw the entire next bar to height 8. Adjacent bins share boundaries; their bars touch. An empty bin has zero height.

The six table rows produce whole bars with heights 2, 8, 7, 4, 2, and 1.

The 10-to-under-20-minute bar represents eight students, not an 8-minute commute. Frequency is on one axis; the commute-time interval is on the other. A horizontal histogram reverses the axes: intervals are vertical and frequencies are horizontal.

Frequency or relative frequency?

For a bin with frequency ff in a sample of size nn,

relative frequency=fn,percentage=fn×100%.\text{relative frequency}=\frac{f}{n},\qquad \text{percentage}=\frac{f}{n}\times100\%.

The second bin has 8/24=1/3≈33.3%8/24=1/3\approx33.3\% of the observations. Every bin uses the same denominator, 24. Scaling all bars by the same factor changes the numerical axis, not their relative pattern.

Frequency counts: 2, 8, 7, 4, 2, 1. Their relative frequencies use denominator 24.

A histogram is not a categorical bar chart

These examples use different illustrative samples: travel method for 40 students, and commute time for 24 students.

Travel method: Walk 8, Bicycle 6, Bus 18, Car 8.

Commute-time frequencies: 2, 8, 7, 4, 2, 1.

Feature Categorical bar chart Quantitative histogram
Data axis Category labels Numerical intervals
One bar One category One interval
Order Unordered categories may move Numerical order must remain
Width Usually a design choice A numerical bin width
Blank space Usually separates categories May indicate an empty bin

Identify the variable and read the axis. Whether bars touch is a useful clue, not a sufficient definition.

NoteWhat is no longer visible?

Height 7 for 20≤x<3020\leq x<30 does not reveal the seven exact times. A histogram usually cannot reconstruct the original observations. Use a dotplot, stem-and-leaf plot, or raw list when exact values matter.

Histogram laboratory

Learning goal: Explain why different histograms can represent exactly the same data.

Change the bins, not the observations

This lab always uses the same 24 commute times. The numerical span remains 0–60 minutes. Choose either a bin width or a number of equal-width bins. Switch the vertical axis between counts and percentages.

Click a bar—or focus it and press Enter—to inspect its interval and recorded values. The width and bin-count controls are alternative ways to specify the same partition.

At width 10 minutes there are six bins with frequencies 2, 8, 7, 4, 2, 1. JavaScript enables the adjustable version.

NoteA fair visual comparison

The frequency scale remains 0–24, or the percentage scale 0–100%. Changing bins does not quietly rescale the vertical axis. Some bars may look short; every graph still represents all 24 students.

Investigate before revealing

1. Compare widths of 5, 10, and 20 minutes. Which view shows more local detail? Which could conceal gaps or separate concentrations?

Show explanation

Width 5 gives 12 bins; width 10 gives 6; width 20 gives 3. Narrow bins show more local detail but can look irregular. Wide bins smooth over detail and may hide gaps or multiple concentrations. No single appearance tells the whole story.

2. With width 10, inspect the third bin. Does height 7 mean seven students recorded exactly 20 minutes?

Show explanation

No. The interval contains 20, 20, 22, 24, 25, 26, and 28 minutes. Only two values equal 20. The panel’s exact values come from the underlying data supplied to the lab—not from the bar itself.

3. Change the number of bins to 9. What changes, and what stays fixed?

Show explanation

The width becomes 60/9≈6.66760/9\approx6.667 minutes. Boundaries, counts per bin, and appearance change. The 24 recorded values, minimum 5, and maximum 58 do not. Boundary labels may be rounded; assignment uses unrounded boundaries.

A gap can disappear from the histogram

This separate clinic dataset has concentrations near 5–12 and 24–30 minutes and one unusually long wait of 55 minutes.

No clinic times lie strictly between 12 and 24 minutes, or between 30 and 55 minutes.

Twenty-minute bins give counts 10, 9, and 1. All bins are nonempty despite the gaps in the raw data.

A zero-frequency bin establishes that its entire interval contains no observations. The reverse does not hold: nonempty bins may contain smaller empty regions. Coarse bins can hide gaps visible in individual-value displays.
