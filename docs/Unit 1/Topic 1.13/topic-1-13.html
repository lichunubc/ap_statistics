Topic 1.13 Experimental Design

An engineer wants to compare two protective coatings for metal panels. The explanatory variable is coating type; the response variable is wear after a standardized abrasion test, measured in milligrams of material lost. A convincing experiment must compare treatments while separating the coating’s effect from other influences on wear.

Four ingredients of a well-designed experiment

A well-designed experiment uses comparison, random assignment, replication, and direct control of important extraneous sources of variation.

Ingredient In the coating experiment Why it matters
Comparison Test Coating A and Coating B under the same abrasion protocol. Provides a reference for judging a treatment difference.
Random assignment Use chance to determine which panel receives which coating. Helps balance other influences between treatment groups.
Replication Apply each coating to several distinct panels. Reveals panel-to-panel variability and makes comparisons more informative.
Direct control Use the same equipment, test duration, pressure, and measurement procedure. Prevents avoidable differences in testing conditions from obscuring the coating comparison.

The experimental units are the individual panels that receive treatments. The treatments are the two coating protocols. Applying a coating repeatedly to the same panel without independently assigned experimental units is not replication of experimental units.

Replication is not measurement repetition. Testing several independently assigned panels replicates the treatment on experimental units. Taking several readings from one panel may improve measurement precision or reveal instrument error, but it does not create new experimental units.

Optional enrichment: SD versus SE

The standard deviation (SD) of observations describes variation among the measured responses. The standard error (SE) of a statistic describes how that statistic would vary across repeated random samples or treatment allocations. For nn independent, comparable observations, the estimated standard error of the mean is often s/ns/\sqrt{n}; repeated instrument readings from the same panel should not be counted as independent panels in this formula. This is a conceptual preview, not a required significance-test calculation.

A control group is a comparison group, not necessarily a group receiving nothing. For a trial comparing a new coating with an established standard, the standard coating can be the control. Multiple active-treatment groups may also be compared.

Extraneous variables and confounding

An extraneous variable is a variable other than the explanatory variable of interest that may affect the response. In the coating study, the production batch and initial surface roughness could influence wear.

A confounding variable is related to the explanatory variable in a way that makes it difficult to distinguish the explanatory variable’s effect from the confounder’s effect. For example, if all Batch 1 panels receive A and all Batch 2 panels receive B, a measured difference could reflect either coating or batch.

Not every extraneous variable is automatically a confounder. Random assignment reduces systematic connections between treatments and other variables; it does not physically remove all variation or guarantee perfectly equal groups in a single experiment. Direct control keeps feasible non-treatment conditions constant.

NoteA crucial distinction

Random selection chooses units from a population and helps with generalization when the sampling design supports it. Random assignment allocates treatments among participating units and supports a causal comparison. These are different randomization steps.

Blinding, placebos, and the placebo effect

Blinding, or masking, concerns who knows the treatment allocation. In a single-blind experiment, either participants or the research team members who interact with them are unaware of assignments. In a double-blind experiment, both groups are unaware. Say explicitly which people are blinded; the words alone can be ambiguous outside a defined study.

Blinding can reduce expectation-driven behavior and assessment bias. A placebo is an inactive treatment designed to resemble the treatment of interest. The placebo effect is the difference between the average response to a placebo and the average response to no treatment, under comparable conditions.

A placebo is not the same thing as blinding: placebos can make blinding practical, but an experiment can include a placebo without successfully masking assignments.

Consider a hypothetical add-on cancer-treatment trial. Every consenting patient continues to receive established standard care. A random allocation compares standard care plus Drug X with standard care plus a matching placebo. Nothing in this example replaces effective care with an inactive pill. The outcome might be change in a measured tumor response after 12 weeks. Any real trial also requires appropriate consent, oversight, and safety review.

Three designs to recognize

Completely randomized design

Start with one pool of experimental units. Assign the treatments to units entirely at random. For 24 panels, label the panels 01–24, randomly select 12 distinct labels for A, and assign B to the remaining 12. This is a completely randomized design (CRD) with fixed group sizes. No blocks are formed.

Randomized block design

If manufacturing batch is known to influence wear, first divide the 24 panels into 12 from Batch 1 and 12 from Batch 2. Within each batch, randomly select 6 panels for A and assign the remaining 6 to B. Every block contains both treatments.

Blocking deliberately groups units with similar values of a relevant variable. It separates between-block variation from the comparison of treatments within blocks. It does not require identical block sizes in general, and it is not sufficient merely to create groups: treatments must still be randomly assigned within each block.

TipWhich design is more precise?

When batch contributes substantial response variation, comparing treatments within batches often makes the treatment effect easier to detect than pooling all panels. Blocking need not help if the chosen variable is unrelated to the response; a particular randomized experiment can still give an unusual result.

Matched pairs design

A matched pairs design is a special randomized block design with two treatments. Each block is a pair of similar units, and one member is randomly assigned to each treatment. Alternatively, each unit can receive both treatments, with the order randomized when appropriate.

Suppose 20 typists test Keyboard A and Keyboard B using comparable passages. Randomly assign 10 typists to try A then B and 10 to try B then A. Each typist provides a paired comparison; differences in baseline typing speed are largely controlled by the matching. Order effects are a separate concern: practice or fatigue may affect the second trial, and residual carryover cannot always be removed simply by randomizing the order.

For paired numerical responses, calculate one difference per pair. If the question asks about B minus A, consistently define di=Bi−Aid_i=B_i-A_i, then describe the mean d¯\overline d. Do not treat the two observations on a person as two independent experimental units.

What conclusions can we make?

A well-implemented, randomized experiment reduces the potential for confounding and allows a cause-and-effect conclusion for the experimental units and the specific treatment conditions studied. Replication, direct control, and appropriate masking improve the quality of the evidence.

Generalization is a separate question. If the tested panels came only from one supplier, random assignment does not prove the coating behaves identically on every possible metal or in every environment. Similarly, human clinical trials often enroll consenting volunteers rather than a random sample of all people; results must be applied cautiously beyond participants.

A study can therefore support a treatment effect among its participants without proving a population-wide effect. Conversely, a random survey can describe a population without assigning a treatment or demonstrating causation.

ImportantEthics and interpretation

Randomly selecting someone for an invitation is not the same as forcing enrollment. For human experiments, participants ordinarily need informed consent and appropriate safeguards. A causal comparison that is unethical or badly measured is not a sound study.

Check your design decisions

  • Name the experimental units, treatments, response variable, and comparison group.
  • Say how chance is used to assign treatments; “split into two groups” alone does not establish randomization.
  • Explain the blocking variable and how treatments are randomized within every block.
  • Explain whether matching uses two people in a pair or two treatments on the same person.
  • Distinguish extraneous variation, confounding, measurement error, and replication.
  • For scope, address both causation (random assignment) and generalization (selection/eligibility and study conditions).
