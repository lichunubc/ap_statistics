Topic 1.12 Potential Problems with Sampling

A school wants to estimate the mean one-way travel time for all its students. Interviewing a large number of convenient volunteers can still give a misleading answer. Before interpreting a sample statistic, ask three questions: Who had a chance to be selected? Who answered? Was the answer measured accurately?

Sampling variability and bias

Sampling variability means that a statistic changes from one random sample to another. Even a properly selected simple random sample (SRS) can have a sample mean above or below the population mean. A single unusual result does not prove that the method is biased.

Bias means a systematic tendency of a selection or measurement process to produce values consistently too large or consistently too small relative to the parameter being estimated. A biased method can remain biased even after many observations.

Situation Main issue
A randomly selected sample has an unusually large mean. Sampling variability may explain the individual result.
An eligibility list omits students learning remotely. Undercoverage.
A public website lets anyone click to vote. Voluntary response.
A researcher surveys only the first people entering a shop. Convenience sampling.
Some selected people never return the survey. Nonresponse.
A question suggests that one answer is socially desirable. Response bias.

Five sources of bias

Voluntary response bias. Respondents choose themselves into a sample, often because they have strong opinions. An open online poll is not an SRS of the general population merely because it receives thousands of responses.

Undercoverage bias. Some population members are omitted or less likely to be selected because of the sampling frame or procedure. A random selection from a landline directory cannot include people who have no landline.

Nonresponse bias. People who were selected fail to provide answers. If responders and nonresponders differ in a relevant way, estimates may be biased. The rate of nonresponse is a useful diagnostic, not proof of the direction of bias.

Response bias. Answers or recorded measurements tend to differ from the true value. Leading wording, interviewer influence, reluctance to report sensitive facts, and an incorrectly calibrated device can all produce response bias. A complete response rate does not prevent it.

Convenience-sample bias. Sampling those easiest to access can favor certain groups, such as early arrivals or customers near one entrance. Randomness must enter the selection mechanism; choosing a large number of readily available people does not make the sample random.

TipQuick diagnosis

A person not on the frame suggests undercoverage; on the selected list but absent from the data suggests nonresponse. Opting into an open survey suggests voluntary response. Present but answering inaccurately suggests response bias. More than one problem may occur in the same investigation.

A numerical example: more observations do not fix exclusion

Consider a model school with 100 students: 40 walkers whose commutes are 10 minutes and 60 bus riders whose commutes are 30 minutes. Its population mean commute time is

μ=40(10)+60(30)100=22 minutes. \mu=\frac{40(10)+60(30)}{100}=\boxed{22\text{ minutes}}.

A researcher interviews only the walkers near a particular gate. Whether the researcher interviews 10, 20, 30, or all 40 walkers, their sample mean is 10 minutes, which is 12 minutes lower than the population mean. That method excludes every bus rider. More interviews from the same gate do not correct the undercoverage.

An SRS drawn from the complete 100-student roster, without replacement, has sampling variability instead. The mean of such sample means across repeated draws is 22 minutes, although a particular sample may be higher or lower. Increasing sample size does not guarantee that the next individual estimate moves closer to 22.

Diagnose the study, then propose a repair

Example 1. An accurate random-number generator selects from a school roster that excludes online students. The random procedure can still suffer from undercoverage. Rebuild a complete eligible roster before selecting.

Example 2. A random sample of 120 students is selected, but 35 selected students cannot be reached. This is nonresponse, not undercoverage of the sampling frame. Follow up using multiple appropriate contact methods and describe the missing responses.

Example 3. Everyone answers, but the interviewer asks, “Surely you support our excellent new plan?” The question may create response bias. Replace it with a neutral question that presents answer choices without favorable adjectives.

Example 4. A voluntary poll receives 20,000 votes. High participation does not turn self-selected responses into a random sample; the result need not represent all eligible people. The direction of bias cannot be determined from the poll size alone.

Check your reasoning

  • A larger sample can reduce random sampling variability without repairing biased selection.
  • A randomly selected sample does not have to match population proportions exactly.
  • A nonrespondent was selected but not measured; an uncovered person could not enter the frame.
  • Response bias concerns the answers obtained, not simply the number of missing answers.
  • Different problems can coexist, and the direction of their effect often requires further information.
