Topic 1.10 The Investigative Question Revisited and Data Collection

A statistical investigation begins before the first graph is drawn. We must decide what information would answer the question, from whom it should be collected, and what conclusions the design can justify. Consider a school that wants to know whether a short morning mathematics review improves students’ next quiz scores.

1. Turn curiosity into a statistical question

“Does review help?” is too vague to investigate. Who is being studied? What counts as “help”? Will students choose whether to attend, or will the school assign sessions? A useful investigative question has three linked components.

Variables and population. Specify the observational units, explanatory and response variables when relevant, and the population of interest. Here the observational unit might be one grade 10 student, with next quiz score (points) as the quantitative response variable.

Statistical purpose. Decide whether the aim is to estimate a population characteristic, compare groups, or evaluate a claim about a parameter. An estimate of the proportion willing to attend is different from a test of whether assigned review increases the population mean score.

Intended conclusion. Identify the group to which results should apply and whether the claim is descriptive, associational or causal. Study design limits conclusions even when observed differences are large.

Investigative question What must be measured? Purpose
What proportion of current grade 10 students at this school would attend morning review? Willingness to attend, yes/no, from students representing that school population Estimate a population proportion
Among current grade 10 students, is the mean next-quiz score greater under assigned review than under assigned no review? Assigned treatment and quiz score in points Compare two population means; evaluate a directional causal claim with an appropriate experiment
Is students’ self-chosen attendance associated with their quiz scores? Natural attendance status and quiz score Assess an observational association, not automatically a causal effect

A population parameter summarizes a population; a sample statistic summarizes the measured sample. If pp denotes the proportion of all current grade 10 students willing to attend, the sample proportion p̂\hat p can help estimate it. A confidence interval seeks a plausible range for pp. The calculation will be developed in a later unit, but the target parameter must be clear now.

For a hypothesis test about the assigned review experiment, let μR\mu_R denote the population mean score under the review condition and μN\mu_N the population mean under no review. The directional question is expressed by

H0:μR−μN=0versusHa:μR−μN>0. H_0:\mu_R-\mu_N=0 \quad\text{versus}\quad H_a:\mu_R-\mu_N>0.

The null hypothesis H0H_0 represents no mean difference; the alternative HaH_a represents higher mean performance under review. A different question—whether the means differ in either direction—would use Ha:μR−μN≠0H_a:\mu_R-\mu_N\ne0. Declaring a hypothesis is not evidence that it is true.

Why these hypotheses? — definition and rationale

The contrast μR−μN\mu_R-\mu_N represents the difference between population means. Equality corresponds to a difference of zero; the claim that review increases the mean corresponds to a positive difference. The sign is chosen to match the investigative question. These are definitions of competing claims, not a theorem requiring a proof.

Parameter: the population proportion willing to attend.

For example, knowing how many students want to attend does not answer whether review causes higher scores. An effect question needs an appropriate assigned treatment and a measured outcome; an association question records naturally occurring behaviour.

2. Population, census and sample

The population is the entire group the question targets. A census records the variable for every member of that group; a sample records it for some members. If a school has 240 grade 10 students, recording all 240 is a census of that school’s current grade 10 population, but not of every student in the district. Recording 60 of the 240 is a sample.

A census need not be perfect: nonresponse and measurement errors remain possible. Likewise, a large sample is not necessarily representative if the selection process systematically excludes some people. The procedures for random sampling and sampling bias are examined in Topics 1.11 and 1.12.

Check your understanding. A researcher asks every student in one school’s basketball team about sleep. This is a census of that team, but is not a census of all students at the school.

3. Observational studies and experiments

The most important distinction is whether the researcher imposes a treatment, not whether the researcher collects numerical data, follows people over time, or asks for volunteers.

Observational studies

An observational study records naturally occurring variables without assigning the explanatory conditions. Investigators could ask students whether they voluntarily attend review sessions and compare their later quiz scores. Attending may be related to score, but this design by itself does not isolate a causal effect of review.

A survey is an observational study that collects answers from people using a standard set of questions. A prospective study selects its observational units at a point in time and gathers information then and into the future, whereas a retrospective study selects units now and gathers information from the past, perhaps using records. Both can be observational: simply observing people for many months does not turn a study into an experiment.

A confounding variable provides an alternative explanation for an observed association. Prior mathematics preparation might affect both students’ decision to attend review and their quiz scores. It is a plausible confounder because it relates to both the explanatory and response variables, not merely because it affects scores.

Experiments

An experiment deliberately assigns conditions, called treatments, to experimental units and measures outcomes. The experimental unit is the smallest unit receiving a treatment; if individuals receive it, they may be called subjects or participants. For a review-session experiment where students are assigned separately, each student is an experimental unit.

A factor is an explanatory variable deliberately manipulated; its different settings are levels. The treatment is a factor level or a combination of levels across factors. In a two-factor experiment with review (yes/no) and practice format (paper/digital), there are four treatments, not two:

Paper practice Digital practice
Assigned review Review + paper Review + digital
No review No review + paper No review + digital

The response variable is the observed outcome measured after treatment, for example next-quiz score in points. Treatment assignment and the response are different variables.

Random assignment uses chance to allocate treatments among units. It makes systematic selection into treatment groups less likely and supports a cause-and-effect interpretation when the rest of the experiment is sound. It does not guarantee that the groups are identical on every characteristic.

Random selection, in contrast, uses chance to choose observational units from the target population. Random selection addresses to whom results may be generalized; random assignment addresses whether a treatment caused an observed difference. A volunteer experiment can randomly assign treatments without randomly sampling the wider population.

4. What can your design conclude?

Ask two separate questions: How did participants enter the study? and How were treatments allocated? For sound designs with appropriate measurement and response rates, these decisions give the following broad guidance.

Randomly selected from target population? Treatments randomly assigned? Appropriate conclusion
Yes Yes Population generalization and a causal treatment comparison may both be justified.
Yes No Generalize measured descriptions or associations; causation still needs justification.
No Yes A causal comparison can apply to participants and similar people, but not automatically to the entire population.
No No Describe participants and observed associations; broad generalization and causal claims are limited.

In an observational study no treatment is assigned, so the random-assignment question does not apply. Some experiments impose treatments without random assignment; pre-existing differences may then undermine a causal conclusion.

The first supports population descriptions, the second helps assess a causal effect among people like the volunteers, and the third can support both conclusions, subject to the study’s other conditions.

Further design considerations

Randomization does not repair poor measurement, substantial nonresponse, failure to follow assigned conditions, or serious attrition. Ethical research requires informed consent as appropriate, privacy safeguards, accurate recording, reasonable risk, and fair treatment of participants. Sometimes an experimental treatment would be unethical or infeasible; an observational investigation may then be the suitable design.

5. Worked AP exam example

The released 2024 AP Statistics Free-Response Question 3 asks students to distinguish an observational car-mileage study from a randomized driving comparison, then evaluate generalization. Read the original question directly below, followed by its worked explanation.

College Board · 2024 AP Statistics · Question 3, pp. 7–8.

Accessible text summary of the original question

Researchers ask owners of four car models to estimate gas mileage. A Model D owner, James, then compares mileage on 70 commuting days, randomly assigning autopilot to 35 days and manual driving to the other 35. Finally he asks whether the result applies to all Model D cars in his club.

Part (a): This is an observational study, because researchers record existing car ownership and mileage without assigning car models to people.

Part (b): Label the 70 commuting days 11 through 7070. Use a random-number generator to select 35 distinct labels without replacement; those days receive autopilot driving, while the other 35 receive manual driving. This randomly assigns two treatments to days (the experimental units). Merely alternating days would not be random.

Part (c): James’s car was not randomly sampled from the Model D club’s cars. To generalize to the club’s population, he should randomly select club-owned Model D cars and conduct a suitable experiment on those sampled cars. Random assignment of treatment days cannot substitute for random selection of cars.

The lesson of the question is to describe selection and assignment in different sentences before making a population or causal claim.

6. Synthesis

Before collecting data, check the target population and observational units; the variables to be measured; whether the goal is estimation, testing or association; whether the study is a census, observational investigation or experiment; any assignment and selection mechanisms; plausible confounders; and the ethical and practical limits of the conclusions.
