Topic 2.4 Introduction to Probability

Probability describes how likely an event is to occur on one trial, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). We calculate theoretical probabilities from a model and check that its assumptions match the process.

The sample space and events

The sample space, denoted SS, is the set of all possible nonoverlapping outcomes of a complete random trial. Exactly one outcome occurs on each trial. An event EE is a subset of these outcomes. It can contain one or several different outcomes.

A single fair-coin toss has S={H,T}S=\{H,T\}. A fair six-sided die has S={1,2,3,4,5,6}S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}. For two fair coin tosses, recording their order, S={HH,HT,TH,TT}S=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}. The outcome HTHT is not the same as THTH.

For one die roll, the event even is E={2,4,6}E=\{2,4,6\}. The entire sample space is certain, so P(S)=1P(S)=1. A probability cannot be negative or greater than 1:

0≤P(E)≤1. 0\le P(E)\le1.

When outcomes are equally likely

If each elementary outcome in the sample space is equally likely, count the outcomes in the event and divide by the number in the sample space:

P(E)=number of outcomes satisfying Enumber of outcomes in S. P(E)=\frac{\text{number of outcomes satisfying }E}{\text{number of outcomes in }S}.

For one fair die, there are six equally likely faces. The event roll at least 5 consists of {5,6}\{5,6\}, so

P(at least 5)=26=13. P(\text{at least }5)=\frac{2}{6}=\frac13.

For two independent fair dice, there are 36 equally likely ordered pairs. The sum is 7 for (1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1), so P(sum=7)=6/36=1/6P(\text{sum}=7)=6/36=1/6.

Why we cannot simply count the possible sums

Although sums 2 through 12 give eleven possible labels, the labels are not equally likely. The sum 2 appears in just one ordered pair, while sum 7 appears in six. The count-over-total rule applies only after identifying an equally likely sample space.

The complement rule

The complement EcE^c (also E′E' or E¯\overline E) means not EE. The event and its complement cannot occur together and together cover every possible outcome.

P(Ec)=1−P(E) \boxed{P(E^c)=1-P(E)}

If EE is rolling a six, P(E)=1/6P(E)=1/6 and P(Ec)=5/6P(E^c)=5/6. The probability of not rolling a sum of 7 with two fair dice is 1−6/36=30/36=5/61-6/36=30/36=5/6.

In a different situation, if a bus is late with probability 0.180.18, then P(not late)=1−0.18=0.82P(\text{not late})=1-0.18=0.82, provided late and not late describe every possibility.

Possible labels need not be equally likely

Suppose a spinner has three differently sized sectors with P(red)=0.60P(\text{red})=0.60, P(blue)=0.30P(\text{blue})=0.30, and P(green)=0.10P(\text{green})=0.10. Three possible labels do not imply that each has probability 1/31/3. The probabilities sum to 1, but the equally likely condition is not met. We still know P(not red)=1−0.60=0.40P(\text{not red})=1-0.60=0.40.

A probability is a model of long-run chance rather than a fixed quota. A fair die does not have to show exactly one six in the next six rolls. This distinction connects the exact model to simulation estimates in Topic 2.3.

Common errors and strategy

  • Listing outcomes that overlap or failing to include a possible outcome in the sample space
  • Treating the number of labels as the denominator when the underlying outcomes are not equally likely
  • Confusing a complete two-dice trial with one die roll
  • Calculating the event probability when the question asks for the complement
  • Treating a chance like 1/41/4 as a guaranteed success exactly once in the next four trials

For each question, first define one trial and the complete sample space, identify EE, check equal likelihood before counting, and use P(Ec)=1−P(E)P(E^c)=1-P(E) when a complement is easier.
