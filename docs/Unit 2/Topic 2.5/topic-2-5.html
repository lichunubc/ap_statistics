Topic 2.5 Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events are mutually exclusive (also called disjoint) when they cannot happen together in one complete trial. To decide this, look for outcomes that belong to both events.

The intersection: “A and B”

Suppose one fair six-sided die is rolled. Define

A={2,4,6}(even),B={4,5,6}(at least 4). A=\{2,4,6\}\quad\text{(even)},\qquad B=\{4,5,6\}\quad\text{(at least 4)}.

The intersection A∩BA\cap B contains the outcomes satisfying both conditions. Here A∩B={4,6}A\cap B=\{4,6\}, so the joint probability is

P(A∩B)=26=13. P(A\cap B)=\frac{2}{6}=\frac13.

The numerator counts the outcomes in both sets; the denominator counts all six equally likely die faces. Joint probability is not the conditional probability P(A∣B)P(A\mid B): a conditional probability uses the restricted group after the vertical bar.

In a two-way frequency table, the intersection is the count in the cell where the events meet, divided by the entire table total when one observation is selected uniformly. For example, among 80 completed orders, suppose 12 are both mobile orders and hot drinks. The joint probability is 12/80=0.1512/80=0.15, not 12/(all mobile orders)12/(\text{all mobile orders}).

Prove whether the events are disjoint

Events AA and BB are mutually exclusive when no outcome can satisfy both. In the discrete probability models used here, this corresponds to

P(A∩B)=0. \boxed{P(A\cap B)=0.}

For one die roll, let C={1,3,5}C=\{1,3,5\} (odd). Since an outcome cannot be even and odd at the same time, A∩C=⌀A\cap C=\varnothing, so P(A∩C)=0P(A\cap C)=0. By contrast, even and at least 4 are not disjoint: 44 and 66 satisfy both.

Name the complete trial. On two coin tosses, “first toss is heads” and “second toss is tails” can happen together: HTHT belongs to both. Different event descriptions do not automatically mean no overlap.

Worked examples: justify your conclusion

Example 1: One card. A standard deck has 52 cards. Let HH mean a heart and KK mean a king. The king of hearts is in both events:

P(H∩K)=152>0. P(H\cap K)=\frac{1}{52}>0.

Therefore, HH and KK are not mutually exclusive. A Venn diagram would place the king of hearts in the overlapping region.

Example 2: One selected school lunch. Suppose students choose exactly one side, fruit or chips. Define FF as fruit chosen and CC as chips chosen. By the stated rule, no student chooses both sides on the same lunch: P(F∩C)=0P(F\cap C)=0. Thus the events are mutually exclusive. If the cafeteria allowed students to select both, the conclusion could change; check the sampling rule.

Example 3: Two independent tosses. The sample space is {HH,HT,TH,TT}\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}. Let AA be first toss heads, BB be second toss tails. Then A={HH,HT}A=\{HH,HT\} and B={HT,TT}B=\{HT,TT\}. Their intersection is {HT}\{HT\}:

P(A∩B)=14>0. P(A\cap B)=\frac14>0.

They are not mutually exclusive, even though they concern two different tosses.

How to explain a claim

An AP-level justification should define the events in context, identify whether a common outcome is possible, and then give the joint probability as evidence. For instance: The two events are not mutually exclusive because a roll of 4 or 6 satisfies both conditions, so P(A∩B)=2/6>0P(A\cap B)=2/6>0.

When a problem gives a two-way table, first find the both cell; do not confuse it with either row/column total. When a model gives event probabilities directly, an explicitly stated P(A∩B)>0P(A\cap B)>0 proves the events are not disjoint.

The distinction between mutually exclusive and independent events will be developed in Topic 2.7. They answer different questions; do not use “independent” as a synonym for “no overlap.”

Common errors

  • Calling events disjoint merely because their names differ
  • Counting AA and BB separately instead of finding A∩BA\cap B
  • Mixing up joint probability and conditional probability
  • Failing to specify whether one trial is one roll, a pair of tosses, or one selected individual
  • Treating drawn Venn-circle areas as numerical probabilities without supplied counts
